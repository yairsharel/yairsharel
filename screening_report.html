BINA project screening
7 October 2026 · 44 papers screened · 12 read (5 in full) · 3 written up
Every paper in this week's digest was read against one test: is the technology innovative, is there a market need you can point at, and would eighteen months and USD 150-300K get to an MVP or to defensible IP? Nothing here is a verdict on the science. No IP claim is verified - a patent application is unpublished for eighteen months, so YEDA's records and the group itself are still the only way to know.
Candidate 1
A live-cell assay that reads whether a drug is blocking its receptor, with cetuximab already on the board
David Margulies (Chemical and Structural Biology)
pursue now
Margulies's group has built a DNA hairpin that is dark in solution and lights up when it engages a cell-surface receptor. Three ligands are clicked into the loop; swapping the fluorophore changes the colour, the ligand changes the target, the valency changes the affinity. They demonstrate it against the folate receptor, CA IX and EGFR, using a small molecule, a second small molecule and a protein as the three ligands - and then do the thing that makes this commercial rather than clever: they use it to see cetuximab, a marketed antibody, blocking EGFR-EGF in living cells, with no wash step. That is an occupancy assay for antibody discovery, and the affinity was deliberately held low enough for an inhibitor to displace the probe, so the readout was designed for it. All twelve authors are Weizmann, Margulies is corresponding, no company appears anywhere, and the funding is three internal grants and the ISF.
Ligand‐Directed Molecular Beacons as Turn‐On Fluorescent Probes for Sensing Cell‐Surface Receptor‐Ligand Interactions · Angewandte Chemie International Edition · 2026-10-01
What could be owned
Three layers, in descending order of how well the paper supports them. The specific probes - a sequence, a ligand, a valency and a dye against a named receptor - which is what a licensee buys and what the data covers directly. The second-generation long-stem design, a 22-base-pair stem melting at 85 degrees with one ligand and no fluorescein, which stays shut at the membrane and fluoresces only after the receptor drags it inside and a nuclease cuts it: that turns the standard objection to DNA probes in cells, that they get degraded, into the signal. And the broad platform claim - multivalent ligand-appended hairpins as receptor-triggered turn-on probes - which is the most valuable and, on this paper's own evidence, the least secure. See the risk section.
The science
A molecular beacon is an old and very well understood object: a stem-loop of DNA with a fluorophore at one end and a quencher at the other, dark when closed. Conventionally it opens by hybridising to a nucleic acid. Here the loop instead carries three ligands, spaced ten nucleotides apart and attached by azide-alkyne click chemistry to modified thymidines. The closed hairpin holds those ligands close together; opening lets them spread far enough to bridge neighbouring receptors in a membrane nanodomain. So receptor engagement does the work hybridisation used to. The construct is 39 nucleotides, a 7-base-pair stem and a 25-base poly-T loop melting around 50 degrees, with Cy5 at the 5' end, a BlackBerry quencher at the 3', and a fluorescein in the loop whose FRET to Cy5 distinguishes a probe that has opened from one that has been cut. Quenching is 92% for the folate probe and 95% for the EGFR one, and opening gives a 12-fold rise.
Is the group on this line of work?
This is the line of work, and the paper is its strongest statement so far. The group has spent years on pattern-generating fluorescent sensors, artificial receptors for protein surfaces, and synthetic agents that sense or imitate protein function, and Yeda already holds granted US patents from it on earlier sensor chemistry - so the lab discloses and the TTO files. The paper cites their own previous turn-on probes from 2026 and states plainly what was wrong with them: they worked only for particular dye-target pairs. This paper is the answer to their own limitation, which is a better signal than a strong result in isolation. Leila Motiei shares the senior authorship, as on much of the group's sensor work, and the author list includes the Institute's own flow cytometry, microscopy and screening facilities - a group that already works with the people who would have to industrialise this.
Market need
Two questions, and the second is the one worth money. Is the receptor there, in a live unfixed cell - which antibodies answer adequately. And is it occupied, by its natural ligand or by the inhibitor we are developing - which antibodies answer badly, because an antibody reports the presence of protein, not its state. A probe that goes dark when a drug displaces it reports the state directly. The paper's own conclusion lists the applications as inhibitor screening, functional evaluation of therapeutic antibodies, and discrimination between cellular states; the middle one is the business.
Market size
Still not established, and I will not invent a figure - the honest sizing needs a day with the reagent catalogues and a conversation with someone who buys screening assays, and it belongs in the committee presentation rather than here. What the full paper does is sharpen which market to size. The reagent framing undersells it: a wash-free live-cell occupancy assay against EGFR, read on a flow cytometer, is a drug-discovery assay, priced per programme, not a catalogue item priced per vial. Size the live-cell target-engagement assay market, not the fluorescent-probe market.
Competitors and the landscape
The paper names its own competition, which saves the work. Disassembly-driven nanoprobes and guest-exchange strategies are the two existing routes to a modular turn-on platform, and it states their specific weaknesses: the first needs its aggregation equilibria tuned, the second needs excess targeting ligand removed first - which is the wash step this avoids. Their own 2026 quinoline-cyanine probes are the third comparator and the honest one, since the limitation being fixed is theirs. Behind all of it sits the antibody, in every format, and the standard answer for target engagement, which is a labelled-ligand competition assay with a wash. Aptamer-sensing companies are the nearest commercial neighbours and should be named before any committee sees this.
IP position (to be checked in YEDA)
Better than the abstract suggested, and the paper carries three concrete signals. No conflicts of interest are declared and no company appears anywhere on it. The funding is the ISF and three Weizmann internal funds - no industry money, so no partner with rights already attached. And it was submitted on 20 May 2026, which matters: a group that files normally files before submission, so anything on this is likely to be months old and still unpublished, which is precisely the position BINA wants. None of that is proof, because chemistry journals do not require patent disclosure in a conflicts statement. What settles it is YEDA's records, and the question to put to them is narrow: is there a filing covering multivalent ligand-appended hairpins, and does it separately cover the long-stem cleavage-activated design? Note also that publication on 1 October is a public disclosure, which starts clocks where nothing has been filed.
Eighteen months
0-3Settle IP and pick targets, in that order
YEDA checked for filings on the multivalent architecture and on the long-stem design separately. Then two or three receptors chosen for commercial pull rather than for how well they worked, each with a named discovery group that says the occupancy readout would be useful.
Go / no-go: A granted family covering the architecture ends it. So does a target list nobody outside the Institute wants.
2-7Find out whether multivalency is really the mechanism
Monovalent against trivalent, head to head, on each chosen receptor, with the contribution of the membrane environment separated from the contribution of multivalent engagement. The paper says this is undetermined and it decides how broad a patent can be.
Go / no-go: If a single ligand performs as well, drop the platform claim and refile around the specific probes and the long-stem design. Keep going - the assay still works - but the project is worth less and the committee should be told before, not after.
6-14Make it an assay a screening group would run
The EGFR occupancy readout in 96- and 384-well plate format with a Z-factor, a dose-response against cetuximab, and signal stability across a plate and across a freeze-thaw. Run it with G-INCPM, who are already on the paper.
Go / no-go: A Z-factor below 0.5 in plate format stops it. This is the gap the paper leaves and the single most likely place for the project to die.
14-18Put it in a customer's hands
One external discovery group running the assay on their own compounds or antibodies, against whatever they use now, plus a filing strategy covering the probes and the long-stem design.
Go / no-go: A licensee at the table, or shelve it with the data packaged.
Budget shape
150-200K, and the shape is clearer now that the chemistry is known. Oligonucleotide synthesis with alkyne-modified thymidines, click conjugation, two dyes and a quencher, three cell lines - all routine for this lab and none of it expensive. One person for eighteen months is most of the money. The real cost centre is the plate-format work, which needs G-INCPM time rather than reagents, and the external validation, which needs a partner rather than a budget line. No animal work, no GMP, no regulatory path.
Ask the PI
  • Has anything been filed on the multivalent hairpin architecture, and is the long-stem cleavage-activated design covered separately? What were the dates?
  • Your own conclusions say the contribution of multivalency to hairpin opening is undetermined, because m-MBFR activated too. What do you believe is happening? If one ligand is enough, is the platform claim still worth filing?
  • Kozer and Barr from G-INCPM are on the paper. Did anyone run this in plate format, and is there a Z-factor sitting in a drawer?
  • Which receptor would you pick if the choice were commercial rather than scientific? EGFR is the one with the drug programmes attached.
  • How do the probes behave in serum, and what is the shelf life after a freeze-thaw? Fragility is the standard reason DNA probes do not reach a catalogue.
  • Can someone outside your lab make a probe against a new receptor from a written protocol, or does it need your hands?
  • Is anyone already asking you for these - a company, a screening centre, a collaborator?
Why this might fail
The order of the risks has changed now that the paper has been read. The biggest is no longer fragility: it is that the platform may not be a platform. Their own monovalent control activated at the cell surface, and they say outright that the role of multivalent engagement is undetermined - so the broadest and most valuable claim rests on a mechanism the authors have not established. Second is the plate format, which the paper does not attempt and which decides whether this is an assay or a microscopy method; two co-authors from the screening centre and no Z-factor is a conspicuous silence worth asking about. Third, still, is serum stability and shelf life, untested here. And fourth, YEDA may hold a granted family already, which would end it - and is the cheapest of the four to check. Do that one first.

Candidate 2
One tunable knob that doubles the beam energy and steadies it at the same time
Victor Armand Malka (Physics of Complex Systems)
watch
Malka's group accelerated electrons to about 1.5 GeV with a 50 TW laser by guiding it in a plasma channel, and found that the channel's length - set by a motorised iris, not by anything exotic - is the control that decides what comes out. Unguided, the same laser gave under 750 MeV. At the best length they got the highest energy, the best shot-to-shot stability and the highest charge together, which is not the usual trade-off. Stability is the reason laser-plasma accelerators have no paying customers, so a cheap mechanical knob that improves it is the commercially interesting part of this paper, not the gigavolt.
Electron Rephasing in a Truncated Tunable Plasma Channel · arXiv (Cornell University) · 2026-09-29
What could be owned
A method - choosing the plasma-channel length relative to the downstream gas-density ramp in order to rephase the bunch - and a product that embodies it: the axiparabola with its motorised iris, sold with the calibration that says which length to use for a given nozzle and backing pressure. The method is the patent; the calibration is what makes the hardware worth buying rather than copying.
The science
A laser driving a plasma wake loses its electrons two ways: the pulse diffracts, and the electrons slip out of the accelerating phase of the bubble. A preformed channel with an on-axis density minimum fixes the first by guiding the pulse. The insight here is that the channel's end can fix the second. Truncating the channel part-way along the gas jet means the pulse exits into higher-density plasma, the plasma wavelength shortens, the bubble contracts and the bunch is pushed back into the accelerating phase - rephased. Too short a channel and the jump is violent, stranding most of the bunch and leaving a narrow monoenergetic peak; ending near the density down-ramp and the shift is gentle enough to re-accelerate much more of it. The group tuned length from 5 to 14 mm, found 12 mm optimal on a 15 mm nozzle and 8 mm on a 10 mm nozzle, and reproduced the whole trend in three simulation codes.
Is the group on this line of work?
Securely inside the group's line of work and on their own machine - the HIGGINS 2x100 TW laser at the Institute - with Malka as senior author and every affiliation on the paper Weizmann. That is the cleanest ownership position of anything on this week's shortlist. Malka has been among the leading figures in laser-plasma acceleration for two decades, which is the strength and also the caution: a twenty-year output, much of it with LOA and ENSTA in France, is a large prior-art surface and a large set of pre-existing institutional claims to check.
Market need
Nobody buys a laser-plasma accelerator for its peak energy. They do not buy them at all yet, and the reason given is always the same: the beam is not the same twice. A control that improves reproducibility while also improving energy and charge addresses the actual objection. The need is real and narrow - it is felt by the people building these machines, not by their eventual end users.
Market size
Small and countable, and I could not find a published figure for it - there is no laser-plasma accelerator market yet to size. The realistic customer set is a few dozen LWFA beamlines worldwide plus the handful of companies trying to commercialise them, which makes this a low-volume, high-value instrumentation sale rather than a market. Anyone quoting a billion-dollar figure here would be quoting the particle-accelerator market, which this is not.
Competitors and the landscape
TAU Systems is the one named commercial player, planning to sell beam time from 2026 on a machine running 60-100 MeV at 100 Hz - which makes them a customer for a stability module rather than a competitor. The real competition is other answers to the same dephasing problem, and there are several published within the past year: flying-focus and dephasingless schemes, plasma lenses, blast waves, and longitudinal density tapering - the paper cites them itself. That the field is crowded says the problem matters; it also says this answer has to be cheaper or more robust than the others, and the argument for it is that it needs one iris.
IP position (to be checked in YEDA)
Axiparabolas are published and in general use, so the optic is not the invention. The narrower claim - truncating the guide inside the density profile to rephase - may well be novel, and the paper says the role of effective channel length is largely unexplored. Two things have to be checked before anything else: what Yeda holds from this group already, and what LOA/ENSTA hold, because Malka's filings may not all sit in Rehovot. An arXiv preprint from 29 September is also a public disclosure, which starts clocks in some jurisdictions.
Eighteen months
0-2Establish who owns what
Yeda and LOA/ENSTA filings on channel-length and axiparabola control identified; the arXiv disclosure date noted against any filing deadline.
Go / no-go: If the method is already filed by another institution, or the disclosure has foreclosed filing, stop before any hardware is built.
2-9Prove it transfers
The optimal length predicted from nozzle geometry and backing pressure alone, then confirmed on a second laser the group does not own.
Go / no-go: If the optimum cannot be predicted for an unfamiliar machine, there is no product. This is the milestone the project lives or dies on, and it should be reached before the money is half spent.
9-14Measure what a buyer asks for
Shot-to-shot energy and charge jitter over several hundred consecutive shots, at the optimal length and either side of it. The paper reports fifteen-shot means; a customer asks about a morning's running.
Go / no-go: If jitter is not materially better than unguided operation over a long run, the stability claim does not survive and the project is only about energy.
14-18Build one and put it in someone's beamline
A standalone module - axiparabola, motorised iris, control software, calibration map - installed and working at one external site.
Go / no-go: A partner willing to host it, or shelve the hardware and license the method alone.
Budget shape
The full 300K, and it will feel tight. Most of it is beam time and one person; the hardware itself - a custom axiparabola, a motorised iris, control electronics - is tens of thousands. The risk to the budget is the transfer test, which needs access to a laser the group does not control, and that is negotiated rather than bought. An in-kind partner for that step is worth more to this project than extra money.
Ask the PI
  • Has anything been filed on this, by Yeda or by LOA/ENSTA? The arXiv preprint is already public - does that affect what can still be filed?
  • Can the optimal channel length be predicted from nozzle and pressure, or does every machine need its own scan? This decides whether there is a product.
  • What is the shot-to-shot jitter over several hundred shots, rather than fifteen? Do you have the data already?
  • What breaks when the repetition rate goes from 1 Hz towards 100 Hz, which is where the commercial machines are? Does the channel survive it?
  • Who else could host the transfer test, and would they?
  • Is anyone asking you for this - TAU Systems, or any of the beamline groups?
  • Would you rather license the method or sell the module? They are different projects.
Why this might fail
The honest answer is that it may be right and still not worth doing. If the optimum is specific to HIGGINS, there is no product. If the crowded field produces a better answer to dephasing in the next two years, the module is obsolete. And even if everything works, the customer set is a few dozen laboratories, so the licensing revenue may never repay the project at the scale Weizmann needs - which is why this is a watch and not a pursue. The right next step is not money but an hour with Malka and twenty minutes in YEDA's records.

Candidate 3
An excellent market reached by a route that is probably already closed
Tsvee Lapidot (Immunology & Regenerative Biology)
watch
Read from the abstract only - no full text was available. Treat the detail below as provisional.
Lapidot's group shows that primitive haematopoietic stem cells run a nightly programme - melatonin peaks, mitochondrial membrane potential falls, DRP1 and PINK1 drive mitophagy, and long-term repopulating capacity improves. If that transfers to a human graft it points at something valuable: harvest or condition at the right time of day and get a better transplant. The problem is the route. Melatonin is off-patent and sold in supermarkets, and a randomised placebo-controlled trial of melatonin plus filgrastim in autologous transplant has already been published, reporting both a higher CD34 yield and faster neutrophil engraftment. The obvious claim is very likely blocked by that paper. What it did not test is timing, and that is the only part still worth examining.
Melatonin, circadian metabolism, mitochondrial remodeling, and cell size dynamics in primitive hematopoietic stem cells · Blood · 2026-09-21
What could be owned
Not melatonin. Possibly the timing of apheresis relative to circadian phase, as a method of obtaining a better graft - which is a protocol, and protocols are hard to own and harder to enforce. Possibly a specific agent acting on DRP1, PINK1 or FoxM1 to reproduce the night state on demand, which is ownable and is a drug-discovery programme several times longer and more expensive than BINA funds.
The science
Two programmes running on a daily cycle, with different drivers. Mitochondrial remodelling tracks melatonin: at night the membrane potential drops, reactive oxygen species rise, DRP1 and PINK1-dependent mitophagy is activated, and the cells that have been through it repopulate better. Glycolytic reprogramming tracks systemic cues instead - feeding and Wnt/beta-catenin - with raised glucose uptake, GLUT1, AMPK phosphorylation and HIF1-alpha. FoxM1 and DRP1 handle quality control while PGC1-alpha drives compensatory biogenesis, and the whole thing is accompanied by changes in cell and nuclear size linked to lamin A/C phosphorylation. It is a detailed mechanism, and the detail is where any remaining patentable position would have to live.
Is the group on this line of work?
Lapidot is last and corresponding author, and the group's standing in this field is not in question - its work on stem-cell homing and the CXCR4 axis is part of the foundation of how mobilisation is understood clinically. On the specific question BINA has to answer, that cuts both ways. A group with thirty years in HSC mobilisation has a large and well-exploited IP history, so the chance that the commercially obvious angle is both novel and unclaimed is lower here than for a group new to its own subject. The 25 other papers pulled for this dossier confirm the circadian and metabolic line is recent and real rather than a one-off.
Market need
Between six and twenty-three per cent of patients fail to mobilise enough CD34+ cells, and the figure approaches thirty per cent on G-CSF alone. Each failure is a repeat procedure or an abandoned transplant. Separately, ex vivo gene therapy is bottlenecked on graft quality, where the economics per patient are very different. Both needs are real, named and expensive - this is the strongest market on the shortlist by a wide margin.
Market size
About 90,000 first transplants a year worldwide, roughly half autologous. The right commercial comparator is plerixafor, the existing mobilisation adjunct, whose market is put at 1.8 to 2.2 billion dollars and growing around nine per cent a year. That figure is what a patentable mobilisation agent is worth. It is not what a timing protocol is worth, and the gap between those two numbers is this candidate's whole problem.
Competitors and the landscape
Plerixafor is the incumbent and the benchmark. Motixafortide and the MGTA-145 class are the named next generation, each a patentable molecule with a sponsor behind it. Against that, an unpatentable supplement given at a particular hour is not a product, however well it works - and the published trial means a competitor can already use it.
IP position (to be checked in YEDA)
This is the one place where the published record says something definite, and what it says is unfavourable. A randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial of melatonin with filgrastim in autologous transplant recipients is in the literature, reporting 6.54 against 4.22 CD34 cells/kg x10^6 and neutrophil engraftment at 11.7 against 12.7 days. That is public prior art for the broad method of use. It does not touch circadian timing of the harvest, and it does not touch the DRP1/PINK1/FoxM1 axis. Whether anything narrow survives is a question for a patent attorney with the full paper in hand, and the full paper is behind Blood's paywall.
Eighteen months
0-2Settle the IP question before anything else
A freedom-to-operate and novelty read on circadian-timed apheresis and on the named mitophagy axis, against the published trial, done with the full paper and YEDA's records.
Go / no-go: If nothing narrow is patentable, stop here. This is the whole project risk and it costs two months and a legal opinion to resolve, which is the right order to do things in.
2-10Does timing transfer to humans at all?
Mobilised human grafts compared by the circadian phase at which they were collected, measuring the quality markers the paper identifies, using material from routine collections at an Israeli centre.
Go / no-go: If graft quality does not vary with collection time in humans, the clinical premise is wrong and the project ends - whatever the mouse data says.
10-18Is the effect worth a schedule change?
An effect size large enough that a transplant unit would reschedule apheresis for it, with a view from one unit on whether they actually would.
Go / no-go: A clinician who says they would change practice, or stop.
Budget shape
Around 150K, and unusually front-loaded with legal rather than laboratory cost. The clinical sample work is observational on material already being collected, which is cheap; the patent opinion is not, and it has to come first. If the IP read is negative the project closes having spent a small fraction of the budget, which is the best argument for sequencing it this way.
Ask the PI
  • Are you aware of the published randomised trial of melatonin plus filgrastim? Does it change what you think is novel here?
  • Has anything been filed - by Yeda or anyone else - on circadian timing of harvest, or on the DRP1/PINK1/FoxM1 axis in HSCs?
  • Does the circadian effect appear in human cells, or only in mouse? The abstract says primitive HSCs without always saying which.
  • How large is the difference in long-term repopulating capacity - two-fold, or ten per cent? The number decides whether any clinician would act on it.
  • Could a drug reproduce the night state at any hour, and is anyone working on that?
  • Is there a collaborating transplant unit that would let us look at collection time against graft quality in their existing records?
Why this might fail
It probably fails on IP rather than on biology, which is unusual and is the reason it is written up despite the recommendation. The market is the best on this week's list; the science looks solid; and the one thing BINA needs - something ownable - is the thing most likely to be missing, because the obvious version of it was published by somebody else four years ago. Spend two months and a legal opinion finding out. Do not spend more until that comes back.
