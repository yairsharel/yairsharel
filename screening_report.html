Candidate 1 · pursue now
A live-cell test of whether a drug is actually blocking its receptor
David Margulies (Chemical and Structural Biology) · Angewandte Chemie International Edition
The problem
To learn whether a candidate antibody is engaging its target on a living cell, you label a competing ligand, add it, wash the cells, and infer occupancy from what is left. The wash costs time, disturbs the equilibrium being measured, and loses anything weakly bound. Antibody reagents do not help: they report that a receptor is present, not whether something sits in its binding site.
The need
Every antibody and small-molecule programme has to answer this for every candidate, and answering it badly is how molecules that fail in cells get advanced. The buyers are discovery groups and the screening centres serving them; what they use today is labelled-ligand competition with a wash, or binding measured on purified protein that may not hold in a membrane. No published figure exists for this assay market and I will not invent one - but the market to size is live-cell target engagement, priced per programme, not probes priced per vial.
The solution
A DNA hairpin that stays dark until it engages the receptor: three ligands clicked into the loop, a quencher holding the dye dark while closed, engagement opening it. No wash, because unbound probe never lights up. Swap the dye for colour, the ligand for target, the valency for affinity - and affinity was kept low on purpose so an inhibitor can displace the probe, which is what makes this an occupancy readout rather than a stain. Shown on the folate receptor, CA IX and EGFR, and used to watch cetuximab, a marketed antibody, block EGFR-EGF in live cells. Ownable: the specific probes, the long-stem variant for fast-internalising receptors, and the broad multivalent-hairpin platform. The platform is the valuable claim and the fragile one - their own single-ligand control also lit up, and the paper says multivalency's contribution remains undetermined. If one ligand suffices, the claim shrinks to the probes. That is the likeliest way this fails.
IP  No company on the paper, no conflicts declared, academic funding only, submitted 20 May 2026 - so anything filed is likely months old and still unpublished, which is the position BINA wants. Yeda holds granted patents from this lab on earlier sensor work, so they do file. One question to YEDA settles it.
Ask the PI
  • Anything filed on the multivalent hairpin? Is the long-stem design covered separately?
  • Your single-ligand probe activated too - if one ligand is enough, is the platform claim worth filing?
  • G-INCPM are on the paper. Did anyone run this in plate format, and is there a Z-factor?

Candidate 2 · watch
The knob that makes a laser-plasma accelerator repeatable
Victor Armand Malka (Physics of Complex Systems) · arXiv (Cornell University)
The problem
A laser-plasma accelerator reaches field strengths no conventional machine approaches, in a room rather than a tunnel - and gives a different beam on every shot. That irreproducibility, not peak energy, is why none has a paying customer. The causes are known: the laser spreads out, and the electrons slip out of the accelerating phase of the wave carrying them.
The need
The people who feel this are the ones trying to sell these machines. TAU Systems intends to sell beam time from 2026 on a system running 60-100 MeV at 100 Hz, and a few dozen laboratory beamlines face the same instability. Each is a low-volume, high-value instrument sale rather than a market, and no published market size exists because there is not yet a market - a large figure quoted here would be the conventional accelerator market, which this is not. That smallness, not any doubt about the physics, is why this is a watch.
The solution
Stop the plasma channel part-way along the gas jet instead of running it to the end. The laser exits into denser plasma, the wave shortens, and the bunch is pushed back into the accelerating phase. The control is the channel's length, set by a motorised iris - one moving part. Tuned from 5 to 14 mm, the optimum gave 1.5 GeV where the unguided laser gave under 750 MeV, with the best shot-to-shot stability and the highest charge at the same setting, which is not the usual trade-off. Ownable: the method of choosing channel length against the downstream density ramp, and the module embodying it - axiparabola, iris, and the calibration telling another laboratory which length to use. The calibration is the product; the method is the patent. The likely failure is that the optimum is specific to the Institute's own laser, leaving a result and no product. Several competing answers to the same problem appeared this year, so this one wins only by being cheaper - and it needs one iris.
IP  Cleanest ownership on the shortlist: Malka is senior author, every affiliation Weizmann. But axiparabolas are published and in general use, Malka's twenty years with LOA and ENSTA mean prior filings may not sit in Rehovot, and the arXiv preprint of 29 September is itself a public disclosure.
Ask the PI
  • Anything filed, by Yeda or LOA/ENSTA? Does the arXiv posting limit what can still be filed?
  • Can the optimal length be predicted from nozzle and pressure, or does every machine need its own scan?
  • What is the shot-to-shot jitter over several hundred shots rather than fifteen?
